Give Mac apps an AI agent you control.

Agneta is a free, open-source menu bar app that runs AI agents for the Mac apps you allow. Each app asks first, every task runs in a sandbox on your Mac, and you can watch and stop any of it. The agents run on the AI account you connect once, and no app ever sees your password or key.

For you, with your own AI plan

Let the apps you choose use AI agents without giving up control: you decide what each one may reach, see everything it does, and can stop it.

  • An app has to ask first. You see the tools and folders it wants and whether its code signature checks out, then allow it once or always.
  • You choose which of your plans and API keys each app may use, and can remove its access at any time.
  • You choose how far its tasks reach: Developer access, like Terminal with your passwords and keys kept out, or Contained, its folders only and no internet.
  • Every task shows the app it belongs to, the provider it runs on and what it costs.

For app developers

Ship agent features without building an agent: Agneta runs a proven one with your app’s own tools and handles sign-in, consent and sandboxing. Your users’ own AI accounts pay for it, so there’s no backend and no AI bill.

  • Use the official TypeScript client, or speak the Agent Client Protocol over a local WebSocket from any language. A sandboxed Mac App Store app can do it too.
  • Keep your tools and data in your app, served as an MCP server on loopback. The agent uses them on the user’s account.
  • Ask for exactly what you need: the providers, engines and mode. Find out first what Agneta offers, and ask it to help the user connect a provider.
  • Send a policy with each session: tools, folders, network, approval and limits. Agneta enforces it with sandboxes, or refuses it.
  • Your users can limit your app too: what it spends on their API keys, its tasks at once, its new tasks. Your app sees the limits and is told plainly when one is reached.
  • Usage comes back with every result, and every task leaves a full trace, live or later.

How it works

{{how_steps}}

What stays on your Mac

Agneta has no servers of its own and sends no telemetry. What leaves your Mac is the task itself: its prompts, and what its tools return, go to your AI provider over HTTPS, because that’s how the model sees it.

Your sign-ins
Plans are signed in by your own Codex or Claude Code, which keep and refresh their credentials. Agneta never holds a plan’s tokens, and API keys stay in your Keychain.
Loopback only
Agneta listens on 127.0.0.1 and refuses requests from web pages. Nothing listens beyond your Mac.
Sandboxed engines
Each engine runs under a macOS sandbox profile. Contained, it reaches the task’s workspace, the folders its policy names and what the engine itself needs, and nothing else in your home folder. With developer access it reaches what Terminal does, apart from known credential stores, sandboxed apps’ data and Agneta’s own.
Every command through Agneta
Every engine’s shell commands go through Agneta, each one in the task’s trace. Contained, they stay in the workspace with no internet; with developer access they build with each app’s own caches, never yours.
Traces you keep
Every task leaves a trace of its prompts, tool calls, model calls and decisions, readable only by you and kept 30 days by default.
Updates, once you allow them
Release builds ask before they first check GitHub for updates. The check carries Agneta’s version and nothing of yours.

Add it to your app

Your app is an ACP client and Agneta is the ACP agent. In TypeScript or JavaScript, @agneta/client connects, pairs once, keeps the token, and starts sessions with your own tools and a policy, typed. In any other language, connect to ws://127.0.0.1:47321/acp: any WebSocket and JSON-RPC 2.0 code will do.

Updates

Agneta updates itself with Sparkle. It asks before it first checks GitHub, then checks about once a day and installs an update only when no task is running; Settings → General turns checks or installs off. Homebrew leaves updating to it.

Check a download

Each release lists every file’s SHA-256 in SHA256SUMS.txt, and GitHub records how each was built: gh attestation verify checks a DMG against this repository.
